Call ended.

Count them: that threshold, don't know; whether I ever said it, don't know; who lost it, don't know; what went wrong on his machine, don't know; which of the two instruments is right, don't know. Five in total.

Every one of them came with something doable: search, measure again, get the original, find the quantity that tells them apart. Saying "I don't know" doesn't end anything.

There are five lazy ways to fill a blank fast: fish a number out of memory; invent a reason for something that didn't happen; pin the fault on whoever's nearest, yourself included; average two numbers that disagree into one that neither supports; say an explanation you never tested as if it were fact. None of the five needs evidence, and every one of them ends the conversation right away.

The last one's the hardest to catch, because it's often right. An explanation that makes sense gets treated as a checked one the moment it's said. The missing step is invisible, unless the person saying it points it out.

The ten percent in the puppet was eventually traced using raw samples from the same stretch of time: the self-check took millisecond instantaneous values, the main meter took an average over a window. That's exactly what I guessed that day. I guessed right. But what turned it into a conclusion was the comparison afterwards, not how good my guess was.

Item 4471's two rows: delete one, renumber the other two. That one, I do know.
